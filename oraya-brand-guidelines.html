Overview

A dark room, one bright frame

Oraya teaches students to make AI-assisted film and vertical drama — the same script-to-storyboard-to-video path professional tools use, built for people who are learning it for the first time.

The name

Oraya joins "ora" — a glow, the light a screen throws across a dark room — with "raya," a beam. It's the ray leaving the projector a half-second before it becomes a picture: raw energy, about to become a story. Treat that image as the brand's center of gravity — everything visual comes back to a dark space with one lit thing in it.

Who it's for

Students who've never touched a camera or a timeline, learning to direct anyway. They're ambitious — they want work that reads as global-standard, not a student project. The brand has to feel like a real filmmaking tool that happens to be teaching them, never like a toy.

Confident like a tool built for professionals. Warm like a classroom. Never precious about either.

Personality

How Oraya behaves

Four traits, held in tension on purpose — a brand that leaned fully into any one of them would stop being useful to a beginner or stop feeling credible to a professional.

Exact

Says what a setting does, what a shot costs, what a model produces. No vague marketing words where a real number or term belongs — students are learning the actual vocabulary of the craft.

Unintimidated

Treats a first-time filmmaker as a director, not a hobbyist. Never talks down, never over-explains the obvious, never hides behind excessive encouragement.

Cinematic

Reaches for film's own language — light, grain, frame, cut — before it reaches for generic software words like "dashboard" or "workflow."

Quiet until it isn't

Calm and monochrome by default. Color and motion show up only at the moment something is being created — never as constant decoration.

Say it like this

"Render at 480p while you're drafting the scene, then switch to 4K for the final cut."

"This shot uses two reference images and one voice clip."

Not like this

"Unlock stunning cinematic magic with our revolutionary AI engine!"

"Oops! Something went a little wrong on our end, sorry about that!"

Voice by surface

The four traits above hold everywhere, but the register shifts with what the surface is for — the same way a director talks differently on set than in a lecture hall than at a premiere.

Studio

Terse and technical, like a director's shot note. States what something costs or does, skips the encouragement. "Two references max on this model" — not "great choice, let's add some references!"

Learn

The most patient of the three. Explains why a technique works, not just what button to press, and never implies a question is too basic to ask.

Showcase

Written for an audience that isn't students — a stranger scrolling in from a shared link. Warmer and more third-person than the other two: names the filmmaker, not "you."

Theme

Status

Feedback colors are not surface colors

Studio's aura already lives in warm orange-red territory, which is exactly where most products reach for "error" by default. Oraya doesn't fight that convention by picking a strange color for failure — it fixes the collision structurally: status colors are always flat, never a gradient, and never appear without an icon and a word next to them. The gradient itself becomes the signal. If it's glowing, Studio is making something. If it's flat, it's reporting on something that already happened.

Motion

The aura breathes, everything else cuts

Two different motion languages, never mixed. The aura is the only thing in Oraya that's allowed to move on its own, continuously, with no action behind it — because it's standing in for something actually happening in the background. Every other transition is quick and deliberate: it answers a click, then it's done moving.

Generating — live
2.4s ease-in-out, scale 1 → 1.08, opacity 0.92 → 1, infinite. Settles to a static circle the instant the render completes.
Instant · 120ms

Hover and press feedback — a button dimming, a chip highlighting. Linear or ease-out.

Settle · 300ms

A panel opening, a shot swapping in, a toast arriving. Ease-out — fast start, gentle stop, never a bounce.

Ambient · 2.4s

The aura pulse alone. Nothing else on the aura scale — it's reserved for "this is still working."

Every animation stops for a reduced-motion preference — the aura included. It still shows a solid gradient, it just stops breathing.

UI principles

Rules for the product itself

A

Dark leads, light is equal

Studio opens in Dark by default; Learn and Showcase follow the system setting. Light mode is a first-class token swap, not a fallback — see Theme for the full pairing.

B

Color marks the surface, not the layout

Each surface's one accent — Studio's aura, Learn's blue, Showcase's violet — lives on primary actions and live states only, never on backgrounds, nav bars, or as a wash behind static content. It never crosses into another surface's screens.

C

Mono means real data

The mono face only ever labels something literally measured — a duration, a resolution, a credit count. It never substitutes for a heading or a button label.

D

Corners follow function

Buttons and status chips round slightly, like something you'd tap. Panels, cards, and swatches stay square, like a slate or a color chip. The two shouldn't be confused.

E

Motion answers an action

Things move when a render finishes, a shot gets added, a scrub bar updates — never as a decorative entrance on scroll. Full timing rules live in Motion.

F

Flat, always

No drop shadows, no blur, no soft glows behind panels — anywhere. Depth comes from the ink/surface step and a hairline border, never a shadow. The single exception is the aura itself, which is allowed to glow because it's light, not a panel pretending to float.

G

Never color alone

Every surface and every status pairs its color with a name, an icon, or a position — Studio's aura always sits next to the word "Studio" or "Generating," never expected to carry the meaning by itself. Body text holds WCAG AA contrast against its background in both themes.

Imagery

Brand colors never touch the footage

Everything so far has been about the chrome around the work — panels, buttons, nav. The work itself follows a different rule entirely: a generated shot stays true to whatever the student is actually making. Aura, Blueline, and Velvet never tint, filter, or grade the actual output. A student making a naturalistic drama and a student making a neon thriller should never have their footage nudged toward "Oraya's colors."

The exception

Placeholder states, before anything has rendered, are the one place the aura is allowed inside a frame — a glowing rectangle standing in for a shot that doesn't exist yet. The moment real content arrives, the aura is gone.

Aspect ratio

9:16 is the default frame everywhere in Studio and Showcase — the format the work actually ships in, not a landscape crop of it.

Thumbnail corners

Small radius, around 10px — functional rounding like a button, not the sharp edge of a color swatch.

Usage

Quick reference

Do

Use one surface's accent once per screen, on the thing you want a student to do next.

Don't

Mix two surfaces' colors on one screen, or apply an accent to backgrounds and borders.

Do

Write instructions the way a director would give a note — direct, specific, no hedging.

Don't

Add exclamation points or "revolutionary," "magic," "stunning" to describe a feature.

Do

Let Light and Dark swap only the five theme tokens — background, surface, text, dim text, border.

Don't

Redesign spacing, imagery, or accent colors between modes, or hardcode a color that ignores the current theme.
